The Affordability Journey

Six steps from "I'd like to live here" to "this is what my month actually looks like". Change any input — everything else updates.

Why does parish matter? Jersey land is finite (≈118 km²). Parishes near the harbour, schools and the finance centre command a premium; quieter rural ones cost less but you pay in commute time. Same property type, different postcode — the price gap is the price of convenience.

Why save more? Lenders charge a higher rate when you borrow more of the price (Loan-to-Value). 10% down vs 20% down can shift your rate by 0.5–1.5%, which compounds over 25 years into tens of thousands. Saving the deposit also proves to lenders — and to yourself — that you can stick to a budget.

Compound interest, the hard way. Over a 25-year mortgage you typically pay back roughly double what you borrowed. That extra is interest — the price of using someone else's money. Shorter terms and lower rates save tens of thousands. This is the same maths that helps you in step 6 when you invest.
4

What salary do you need?

Lenders typically cap mortgage at ~4.5× your gross annual salary, or want repayments under ~35% of take-home. We use the stricter of the two.

£—
gross salary required (approx.)
This is why career choice matters. Median full-time pay in Jersey is around £45–50k. To buy in St Brelade you may need finance, law, senior public sector or two earners. The "job ladder" isn't just about ambition — it's about which housing options stay open to you.
5

Your Jersey payslip

Simplified for learning: 20% income tax above the ~£20,500 exemption, 6% employee social security, optional 5% pension.

Where does the deduction go? Income tax funds States of Jersey services — health, education, infrastructure, benefits. Social security funds the States pension, maternity allowance, long-term care. Your pension contribution is paying your future self. None of this is "lost" — but you should know who's deciding how it's spent (that's step 6 of life, called voting).
6

What's left — needs, wants & future

After the mortgage, your take-home funds everything else. A common starting frame is 50% needs / 30% wants / 20% save & invest — adjust to your values.

This split is a choice, not a rule. Push savings up to retire earlier or buy faster. Push wants up to enjoy now. Credit cards (≈24% APR) and personal loans turn "wants now" into "much more, later" — the same compound interest, against you. Step into "Invest" to see what your savings could become.

Property Purchase Prices

Quarterly average prices from Statistics Jersey (2002–2025). Gov.je Open Data

Why did prices move like that? Markets respond to many variables at once — supply, money, confidence and shocks.
  • 2007–08: global financial crisis. Credit froze, transactions collapsed but Jersey held value better than the UK.
  • 2014–19: ultra-low interest rates made borrowing cheap → prices crept up faster than wages.
  • 2020–22: COVID savings + cheap money + remote work → demand spike. Peak £/m² ever recorded.
  • 2022–25: war in Ukraine pushed energy/inflation; central banks raised rates fast; mortgages got expensive; prices cooled.
Whoever tells you "houses always go up" is selling something. Prices are a story about wages, rates, supply and shocks — not gravity.

Renting isn't "wasting money". It's paying for flexibility, no maintenance, and no mortgage interest. The fair comparison isn't "rent vs nothing" — it's "rent vs (mortgage interest + maintenance + opportunity cost of the deposit invested elsewhere)". For young earners moving jobs, renting often wins on the maths and on freedom. The trade-off: you don't capture price growth, and the landlord chooses the kitchen.

The same compound interest that made your mortgage cost double is now working for you. Start earlier and the curve does most of the work — £200/mo for 40 years beats £400/mo for 20. Higher expected returns come with higher chance of loss. Bitcoin can ten-bag, or halve, in a year. Gov bonds barely beat inflation but you keep your money. Choose the mix you can actually sleep through in a bad year.

Who's looking after your interests?

The numbers above aren't just personal — they're political. Tax rates, housing policy, pension rules and benefits are decided by people you elect (or don't).

Questions worth asking any Deputy candidate

  • What's your plan to keep housing affordable for under-35s?
  • Would you change the income tax exemption thresholds — and who benefits?
  • How will the States pension stay solvent as the island ages?
  • Should social housing supply grow, and how is it paid for?
  • What's your position on stamp duty for first-time buyers?

This site is non-partisan. The point isn't who to vote for — it's that the people setting these numbers should be answering for them.
